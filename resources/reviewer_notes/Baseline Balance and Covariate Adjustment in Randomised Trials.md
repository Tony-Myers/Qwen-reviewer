# Baseline Balance and Covariate Adjustment in Randomised Trials

## Purpose

This guide helps reviewers assess baseline comparisons and covariate adjustment in randomised trials. It distinguishes the validity of the randomisation procedure from the amount of baseline balance observed in one realised sample, and distinguishes principled covariate adjustment from post-hoc selection based on baseline significance tests.

## What reviewers should look for

Reviewers should ask:

- Was treatment allocation genuinely random and was allocation concealment adequately protected?
- Are important baseline characteristics described by randomised group?
- Have authors used significance tests of baseline differences to judge whether randomisation was successful?
- Were covariates for the primary adjusted analysis pre-specified?
- Were covariates chosen because they were expected to be prognostic for the outcome, rather than because their baseline group comparison happened to be statistically significant?
- Were variables used in the randomisation or stratification scheme appropriately reflected in the analysis?
- Are any adjusted variables measured after randomisation or potentially affected by treatment?
- If an important chance imbalance prompted an additional adjusted analysis, is that analysis clearly distinguished from the pre-specified primary analysis?

## What does randomisation guarantee?

Proper randomisation concerns the mechanism by which participants are assigned to treatment groups. It protects against systematic selection into treatment groups and balances baseline characteristics on average over repeated randomisations.

It does not guarantee that every realised trial will have closely similar treatment groups on every baseline characteristic. Chance imbalances can occur, particularly in smaller samples.

An observed baseline imbalance therefore does not by itself show that randomisation failed. Conversely, apparently similar baseline characteristics do not prove that the randomisation procedure was implemented correctly. Assessment of randomisation should focus on how allocation was generated, concealed and implemented.

## Should baseline characteristics be significance-tested?

Routine significance testing of baseline differences between properly randomised groups is not recommended.

If randomisation has been properly conducted and not compromised, observed baseline differences are chance differences. A baseline significance test therefore does not provide a useful test of whether randomisation "worked".

A small p-value does not demonstrate failed randomisation. A large p-value does not demonstrate adequate balance.

Baseline characteristics should instead be described by treatment group. Reviewers should consider the magnitude of any observed imbalance and the prognostic importance of the variable rather than using a significance threshold to classify groups as balanced or unbalanced.

## Does baseline imbalance justify covariate adjustment?

Observed post-hoc imbalance alone is not an appropriate reason to add a covariate to the primary analysis.

Covariates for the primary analysis should generally be selected prospectively because of their expected relationship with the outcome, clinical importance, or role in the randomisation design.

If a substantial chance imbalance occurs in an important prognostic variable that was not included in the pre-specified primary model, an additional adjusted analysis may be useful as a sensitivity or exploratory analysis to assess whether the primary conclusion is robust.

This is different from selecting covariates simply because their baseline comparison produced p < 0.05.

## Why adjust for baseline covariates in a randomised trial?

An important reason for covariate adjustment in a randomised trial is improved statistical efficiency.

Adjustment for baseline variables that strongly predict the outcome can reduce residual variation, improve precision, narrow confidence intervals and increase power.

This should be distinguished from adjustment for confounding in a non-randomised study. With properly randomised treatment assignment, the rationale for baseline adjustment is not ordinarily that treatment assignment must be deconfounded using observed baseline variables.

Covariate adjustment can also account analytically for variables used in stratified randomisation and can help assess robustness to important chance imbalances.

## How should covariates be selected?

For a primary adjusted analysis, prefer a small number of well-justified baseline covariates selected using prior clinical knowledge, previous studies, established prognostic evidence and the trial design.

The adjustment strategy should preferably be specified prospectively before comparative treatment data are examined.

Do not select covariates for the primary model merely because:

- their baseline treatment-group comparison is statistically significant;
- they are the variables most strongly associated with the outcome in the current trial dataset; or
- an automated variable-selection procedure happens to retain them.

Post-hoc analyses can sometimes be informative, but they should be identified and interpreted as such.

## What about variables that are not prognostic?

Adjustment does not automatically improve precision.

Covariates that are prognostic for the outcome are the ones most likely to improve efficiency. Adjustment for variables with little prognostic value may provide little benefit and can sometimes reduce precision.

The fact that a variable differs between randomised groups does not by itself establish that adjustment for it will improve the analysis.

## What about variables measured after randomisation?

Variables measured after randomisation and potentially affected by treatment should not ordinarily be treated as ordinary baseline covariates in the primary treatment-effect analysis.

Adjustment for a treatment-affected variable can alter what effect is being estimated and may remove or otherwise distort part of the treatment effect.

Post-randomisation variables therefore require a causal and estimand-specific justification rather than being included as routine covariates.

## Stratification variables

When randomisation was stratified using baseline variables, the analysis should generally account for the stratification variables unless there is a justified reason not to do so.

The analysis strategy should therefore reflect important features of the randomisation design.

## Common misconceptions

### "A significant baseline difference means randomisation failed."

Incorrect.

Proper randomisation does not guarantee exact baseline balance in every realised sample. Chance differences can occur.

### "A non-significant baseline difference proves the groups are balanced."

Incorrect.

Failure to reach a significance threshold does not establish equivalence or absence of a potentially important difference.

### "Adjust every baseline variable with p < 0.05."

Not recommended.

The p-value from a baseline comparison is not an appropriate variable-selection rule for the primary adjusted analysis.

### "Adjustment is necessary to remove confounding created by chance baseline imbalance."

This is generally the wrong framing for a properly randomised trial.

Randomisation determines treatment assignment. Prognostic covariate adjustment is principally used to improve efficiency, reflect the randomisation design, and sometimes assess robustness to important chance imbalance.

### "More covariates always produce a better adjusted analysis."

Incorrect.

Covariates should have a clear rationale. Unnecessary adjustment can provide little efficiency gain, reduce precision, complicate the model and make interpretation more difficult.

## Reviewer red flags

- Baseline p-values used to declare randomisation successful or unsuccessful.
- Covariates included in the primary model solely because p < 0.05 at baseline.
- Covariates chosen retrospectively after examining comparative treatment data.
- Observational-study confounding language used without recognising that treatment was randomised.
- Post-randomisation variables entered as ordinary baseline covariates.
- A large collection of poorly justified covariates included in a small trial.
- Stratification variables ignored in the analysis without explanation.
- A post-hoc adjusted analysis presented as though it were the pre-specified primary analysis.

## Quick reviewer checklist

□ Randomisation validity is assessed from the allocation process, not baseline p-values.

□ Baseline characteristics are described by treatment group.

□ Baseline significance testing is not used to decide whether randomisation succeeded.

□ Primary adjustment covariates have a prospective clinical, prognostic or design-based rationale.

□ Covariates are not selected simply because baseline p < 0.05.

□ Important stratification variables are appropriately reflected in the analysis.

□ Post-randomisation variables are not treated as routine baseline covariates.

□ Post-hoc and sensitivity analyses are clearly distinguished from the pre-specified primary analysis.

□ The rationale and interpretation of adjusted analyses are clearly reported.
